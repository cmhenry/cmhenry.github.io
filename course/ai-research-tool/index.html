§ overview

This is a self-paced course on using AI — specifically large language models — in social science research. It was developed from a three-day block seminar taught at the University of Zurich in September 2026 and expanded into eight standalone modules.

The course does not teach you to use specific AI products. Products change; the methodological questions do not. Instead, it teaches a framework for deciding where, if anywhere, AI should enter your research workflow, under what conditions, and on the basis of what evidence.

Fluency is not truth. A model can produce a perfectly formed paragraph, complete with plausible-sounding citations and confident tone, that is entirely wrong. This is not a bug — it is a direct consequence of how these systems work.

who this is for

  • — MA and PhD students in the social sciences
  • — Researchers who want to use AI thoughtfully rather than optimistically
  • — No programming experience assumed
  • — No commercial accounts, API keys, installation, or payment required

how to use this course

Each module is self-contained and can be taken in any order. The recommended path:

1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → 7 (validation) → 5 → 6 → 8 (synthesis)

You can also take Module 1 (foundations) and Module 7 (validation) first, then pick the application modules that matter to your research. Module 8 is the capstone.

Estimated total time: 24–30 hours (8 modules × 3–4 hours each)

§ the course lens

Every module uses four questions to evaluate AI use in research. Apply them before using AI, after using AI, and when reading research that uses AI.

question 1

What is being delegated?

Name the task and the decision separately. A task delegation ("summarise these three papers") is diferent from a decision delegation ("identify the most important papers in this field").

question 2

What would make the result believable?

Name evidence outside the model output. Did you check the sources? Did a human agree with the labels? Did you run it twice? The model's confidence is not evidence.

question 3

What could be lost or flattened?

Look for variation, minority cases, ambiguity, provenance, and tacit knowledge. Are edge cases being forced into the closest category? Is the source of a claim becoming invisible?

question 4

What must remain visible or human?

Which decision requires a researcher's judgment? What check must be performed by a person? What record must survive so a reviewer can inspect what happened?

§ portfolio

The course culminates in a portfolio that answers one question:

Where, if anywhere, should AI enter my research workflow, under what conditions, and on the basis of what evidence?

You may conclude: integrate, integrate with limits, continue experimenting, or do not integrate. All four conclusions can be equally strong. The grade depends on the quality of evidence and reasoning, not on whether you adopted AI.

constraints

  • — No programming required. Code is demonstrated and provided as scafold.
  • — No commercial accounts, API keys, installation, or payment required for any asessed component.
  • — Every exercise has an equivalent no-account path. Constructed fallback outputs are provided.
  • — Do not upload material you lack the authority to share. Unpublished co-authored drafts, interview transripts, administrative data, and identifiable records stay on your own device.
