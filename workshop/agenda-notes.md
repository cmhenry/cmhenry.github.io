# Agenda — Presenter Notes

**Target time:** ~5 minutes
**Goal of this module:** Set the posture for the day. We are skeptics with leverage, not automators.

---

## Slide 1 — Title: "Agenda"

Brief. Just say hello and frame the next five minutes.

> "Before we touch a tool, let's spend five minutes on why we're doing this — and what I'm explicitly *not* trying to convince you of."

---

## Slide 2 — Why we're here

The core tension. Lean in to it.

- The internet is full of demos that imply you can hand a research question to an AI and get a paper back. That is not what we're doing today.
- Almost every AI tool we'll touch was built by — and for — software engineers. That heritage matters: it shows up in the defaults, the affordances, and the rhetoric ("move fast", "ship", "MVP").
- Research has different defaults: reproducibility, theoretical rigor, slow careful inference. These are not the same defaults.
- The workshop is not anti-AI. It is pro-judgement. We're going to learn how to use these tools without letting their assumptions become ours.

Possible aside: *"If you came hoping I'd tell you to automate your lit reviews — sorry. If you came hoping I'd tell you AI is useless — also sorry."*

---

## Slide 3 — Three goals

Walk these one at a time. Keep it concrete.

1. **Recognize assumptions.** When a coding agent suggests "just run this and see what happens," that's an engineering reflex, not a research one. We'll learn to spot these.
2. **Evaluate workflows.** Traceability (where did this citation come from?), auditability (can a reviewer reconstruct this step?), reproducibility (will this run again next year?). These are the bar.
3. **Use tools practically.** I'll use Claude in demos, but the skills transfer. Models change every six months. What we're learning is a way of working, not a product.

---

## Slide 4 — Healthy skepticism, human in the loop

The posture slide. Slow down here.

- "Draft, not answer" — every output is something you edit, not something you accept.
- The thing you're best at — domain judgement, theoretical framing, knowing what's interesting — is exactly what these tools cannot do. Don't outsource it.
- "If you can't audit it, you can't publish it." Useful test. If a reviewer asked "where did this number come from," would you have an honest answer?
- Frame it as **leverage, not automation**: the goal is to do more of *your* work, not to have someone else do it.

## Slides 5–7 — The adoption gap (three figures)

All three slides come from the [Anthropic May 2026 study](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences) (Lyttelton, Massenkoff, Wilmers; n=1,260 quantitative social scientists, surveyed Feb–Mar 2026).

Open the section with the headline numbers before you click through:

> "81% of social scientists have tried AI chatbots. Only **20%** regularly use coding agents. And that 20% isn't distributed the way you'd hope."

### Slide 5 — Adoption by discipline

- Economists lead (~39%), then political scientists (~25%).
- Public health, education, communications: single digits.
- For this room: political science is ahead of the curve, but it's still a minority — most of your colleagues aren't doing this yet.

### Slide 6 — Adoption by career stage

- Doctoral students and postdocs: ~25%+ adoption.
- Tenured faculty: less than half that rate.
- The juniors are getting there first — more code-fluent, more career pressure.
- Read to the room: if you're senior, this is the gap that matters most for *you*.

### Slide 7 — Adoption by type (gender, institution)

- Researchers with male names adopt at **2×** the rate of those with female names.
- Top-25 universities ~40% more likely than others.
- These gaps are **larger** than the corresponding gaps in general AI use — coding agents are amplifying disparities.
- This is why "just let the field adopt naturally" is not a neutral position.

Close the section with the bridge:

> "The tools have real productivity effects — more working papers, more grant submissions. An uneven adoption gap becomes an uneven career gap. Our job today is to help close it *without* importing the engineering defaults that came with the tools."

## Things to watch for during this module

- Don't oversell. The skeptical framing is the point; if you slip into evangelism here, the rest of the day reads as sales.
- Don't undersell either. People came; they want to learn something useful.
- If someone pushes back ("I think this is all hype"), agree with the *premise* (a lot of it is hype) and redirect to the *practice* (which is what we'll do for the next 2h 55m).
- Watch the clock. This is a 5-minute module. If you're at 7 minutes, cut the adoption-gap slide to one sentence and move on.
